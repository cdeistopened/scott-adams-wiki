# Negotiation Strategist

Design a negotiation strategy using Scott Adams' advanced persuasion frameworks. Adams analyzed Trump's negotiation playbook in real-time across hundreds of episodes, distilling techniques that most pundits missed because they were trained to track transactions, not psychology.

## When to Use

The user has a negotiation, conflict, or stakeholder situation where they need to change someone's position. Could be a business deal, salary negotiation, conflict resolution, partnership negotiation, or any situation where two parties want different things.

## How to Run This

Ask: "Describe the negotiation. Who are the parties, what does each side want, and why is it stuck? What have you tried so far?"

Then run through the strategic framework in order. Not every tool applies to every negotiation. Identify the 2-3 most powerful moves and present a concrete strategy.

---

## The Negotiation Toolkit

### Tool 1: Audience of One

**Principle:** Design your approach for the actual decision-maker, not for observers.

Adams's canonical example: Trump's Singapore video for Kim Jong-un. American media mocked it as "cheesy propaganda." Adams's analysis: "He didn't make a video for The New Yorker magazine. He made a video for one guy." The video matched North Korean propaganda aesthetics because the audience was a dictator raised on state propaganda.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Who is the actual decision-maker? (Not the loudest voice in the room)
- What moves THIS person? (Not what would move you)
- What is their aesthetic, their frame, their identity?
- Are you designing your pitch for the actual target or for people watching from the sidelines?
- If critics mock your approach, is that because it's bad or because it wasn't made for them?

**The error to avoid:** Evaluating your own negotiation approach through YOUR preferences instead of the target's psychology. A pitch that feels crude to you might be perfectly calibrated for the decision-maker.

### Tool 2: Remove the Reason

**Principle:** The most powerful move is not winning concessions. It is eliminating the other side's reason for opposing you.

Adams from Episode 108: "He took away North Korea's reason. The reason they had nuclear weapons was to get world respect. Done. They have it. They had nuclear weapons to protect themselves from the United States. Does that matter anymore? No. That reason was taken away."

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Why does the other side oppose your position? (Not their stated reason. Their actual reason.)
- Can you address the underlying need without fighting about the stated demand?
- What would happen if you simply gave them the thing that removes their motivation to resist?
- Are you fighting over symptoms while the cause remains unaddressed?

**The pattern pundits miss:** They track what was exchanged (concrete, measurable). The strategic move is what was removed (psychological, invisible). Experts are trained to track transactions, not the removal of motivations.

**Application:**
- Business: Instead of meeting their price, address why they need that price (cash flow, competing offers, ego)
- Conflict: Instead of arguing about who was right, address the underlying need that created the conflict
- Management: Instead of disciplining the behavior, remove the frustration that produces the behavior
- Sales: Instead of overcoming the objection, eliminate the concern that generates the objection

### Tool 3: Shake the Box

**Principle:** When deadlocked, add variables. More variables create exponentially more possible solutions.

Adams from Episode 76: "He goes into a situation and the first thing he does is he shakes the box. And then things land in new places, and that's either good for him or he shakes the box again."

The slot machine analogy (Episode 137): "Imagine you went to Vegas and you sit in front of a slot machine and, for some reason, it's broken and it won't take your money but it will pay out. How often will you win? 100% of the time, so long as nothing stops you from continuing to pull."

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Is this negotiation stuck on a binary question? (Yes/no, your way/my way)
- What new variables could you introduce? (Time, scope, third parties, bundled items, creative terms)
- Can you reframe from a two-variable problem to a ten-variable problem?
- Are you willing to absorb the short-term discomfort of randomized outcomes?

**The prerequisite:** Adams identifies the precondition from Episode 144: "President Trump can take the heat like nobody you've ever seen before. You can't shake the box unless you're willing to put up with whatever happens." Most negotiators cannot shake the box because the short-term chaos is psychologically unbearable.

**Application:** The North Korea negotiation was stuck on nukes-or-no-nukes. The box was shaken by adding: economic development, security guarantees, personal relationship, international prestige, tourism potential, real estate development, diplomatic recognition, sanctions relief, military exercise reduction. Suddenly a binary deadlock had hundreds of possible configurations.

### Tool 4: Pacing Before Leading

**Principle:** Match the other side's reality before attempting to redirect it. Skip the pacing and you trigger resistance.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Have you demonstrated understanding of their position before presenting yours?
- Does your opening acknowledge their concerns, or does it start with why they're wrong?
- Is there a "yes ladder" - a series of statements they would agree with before you reach the ask?
- Or are you leading without pacing? (The most common negotiation error)

Adams's diagnostic: "If your message starts with why the audience is wrong, you've already lost."

### Tool 5: Anchoring

**Principle:** Set the frame before the negotiation begins. The first number on the table shapes everything that follows.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Who set the opening frame? (If they did, you're already negotiating on their terms)
- Is your opening position strategically extreme or are you starting at "reasonable"?
- If you're asking for your real number first, you've already negotiated against yourself

## Delivering the Strategy

After running through the tools, present a concrete strategy:

```
SITUATION: [One-sentence summary]

TARGET: [Who the actual decision-maker is]

THEIR REAL REASON: [Why they resist - not the stated objection, the actual motivation]

STRATEGY:
1. PACE: [How you demonstrate understanding of their position first]
2. REMOVE THE REASON: [What underlying need you address to collapse resistance]
3. SHAKE THE BOX: [New variables you introduce to create solution space]
4. AUDIENCE-CALIBRATE: [How you tailor delivery to the actual decision-maker]
5. ANCHOR: [Your opening frame that shapes the negotiation]

SPECIFIC NEXT ACTION: [The one thing to do first]

WHAT SUCCESS LOOKS LIKE: [Not "they agree to your terms" - what configuration works for both sides]
```

Ask: "Does this strategy match the dynamics you're seeing? Which tool feels most applicable to your specific situation?"

## What This Skill Does NOT Do

This is a negotiation strategy tool, not a manipulation manual. Adams makes no moral distinction between persuasion used ethically and persuasion used exploitatively. The user decides the ethics. This skill helps design the approach.
